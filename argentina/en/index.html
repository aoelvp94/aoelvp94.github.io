A guide to enjoying Argentina

Practical tips for friends and visitors, from an Argentine who spent years traveling the world and came back to appreciate what’s good about his country. This is not a formal tourist guide — it’s what I’d tell someone I know who’s coming to visit, now with a map, real travel times from Buenos Aires, order-of-magnitude prices, and cards for the places I actually went to.

Jujuy

Jujuy Province · Northwest

From BA Bus ~20–22 h · overnight Flight ~2 h

The north flips the script: colored hills, Andean towns, and a slower tempo than Buenos Aires. The Quebrada de Humahuaca is UNESCO-listed and it shows.

What to do
Purmamarca and Cerro de los Siete Colores, Tilcara, Humahuaca, Salinas Grandes if the weather plays along. Give yourself time: the altitude is real.
Eat
Humita, tamales, Jujuy-style empanadas, locro, llama on some menus. Api with pastelitos if you hit a market day or winter.
How to get there
Fly to JUJ (Horacio Guzmán) from AEP/EZE, or a long-distance bus from Retiro (~20–22 h). Plenty of people combine Salta + Jujuy by car or tour.
Tours
The classic is Quebrada + salt flats (full day). Salta operators such as Movitrack cover the north; for a Jujuy-based guide, check recent TripAdvisor/Reddit threads before sending a deposit.
Visited by Aldo

Salta

Salta Province · Northwest

From BA Bus ~18–20 h · overnight Flight ~2 h

“Salta la linda” is not empty marketing: colonial centre, hills close by, and a serious table. A comfortable base for the Calchaquí valleys and Cafayate.

What to do
The centre and Cerro San Bernardo, Cachi or Cafayate via Ruta 40/68, torrontés at wineries, and the High Mountain Archaeology Museum if Inca history pulls you in.
Eat
Salta empanadas (juicy, often baked), locro, tamales, goat. A downtown classic is Doña Salta (empanadas and regional food; queues are normal). A folklore peña at night if that’s your thing.
How to get there
Fly to SLA (~2 h from Buenos Aires) or take the bus (~18–20 h). Car or tour for Cafayate/Cachi: distances lie.
Tours
Movitrack runs 4×4 safaris and valley days. The Tren a las Nubes is another famous postcard (check dates: it does not run daily). For Cafayate wineries, going straight to the bodega is often enough.
Visited by Aldo

Tucumán

Tucumán Province · Northwest

From BA Bus ~14–16 h · overnight Flight ~2 h

The smallest province and one of the densest: heat, independence-era history, and a sandwich culture that is local religion.

What to do
Casa Histórica (independence, 1816), downtown San Miguel, Tafí del Valle if you have an extra day, Quilmes Ruins to the west.
Eat
The sánguche de milanesa (the Tucumán classic, on sanguchero bread) and Tucumán empanadas. That, before any “auteur” restaurant. Look for neighborhood sandwich shops, not a gourmet temple.
How to get there
Fly to TUC (~2 h) or bus (~14–16 h) from Buenos Aires. Tafí is about 2 h by car on mountain roads.
Tours
Tafí del Valle and Quilmes work with a local agency or a rental car. I will not pin you to one operator: check recent reviews and that the vehicle can handle the climb.
Visited by Aldo

Santiago del Estero

Santiago del Estero Province · Northwest

From BA Bus ~12–14 h · overnight Flight ~1 h 45

Folklore lives here. Less of an international tourist circuit, more chacarera, long siestas, and hot springs nearby.

What to do
Downtown Santiago, Termas de Río Hondo (a short hop), and if you care about music, ask for peñas. Do not expect Salta-level tourist infrastructure.
Eat
Empanadas, goat, locro, mazamorra. Stew-and-Sunday food.
How to get there
Fly to SDE (~1 h 45) or an overnight bus from Buenos Aires (~12–14 h). Easy to chain with Tucumán.
Tours
The hot springs you can sort on the ground. For the rest, a local pointer beats a packaged tour: there is no must-book operator I can name with a straight face.
Visited by Aldo

Misiones (Iguazú)

Misiones Province · Northeast

From BA Bus ~16–20 h · overnight Flight ~1 h 45–2 h

Jungle, humidity, and the white noise of the falls. Puerto Iguazú is the base; the park is the show.

What to do
Iguazú National Park (upper and lower circuits plus Garganta del Diablo). Cross to Foz do Iguaçu if you have time and papers. Bug spray, water, shoes that can get wet.
Eat
Litoral plus Brazilian/Paraguayan influence: river fish (surubí, dorado when available), cassava, chipá. Park food is pricey — bring snacks.
How to get there
A flight to IGR (~1 h 45–2 h from Buenos Aires) usually beats the overnight bus (~16–20 h). Posadas is closer (~12–15 h by bus). Airport transfers run to town and the park.
Tours
The walkways are self-guided. For a boat under the falls, Iguazú Jungle runs Gran Aventura and other park boat trips (park ticket sold separately). Check times: the river decides.
Visited by Aldo

Entre Ríos

Entre Ríos Province · Litoral

From BA Bus ~3–6 h Depends on the town

Rivers, islands, carnival, and hot springs. Long-weekend Argentina more than an international postcard.

What to do
Hot springs (Federación, Colón, Villa Elisa), Gualeguaychú carnival in season, Palacio San José near Concepción del Uruguay, fishing and small boats on the river.
Eat
River fish, citrus, local salami and cheese. Less guidebook-asado, more family table.
How to get there
Bus from Retiro (about 3–6 h depending on the town) or drive RN14. Occasional flights to Paraná (PRA).
Tours
Hot springs and carnival you handle on the ground. For islands or fishing, hire a local boat the day before and ask your lodging for names.
Visited by Aldo

Rosario

Santa Fe · river / central

From BA Bus ~3–4 h Flight ~1 h

A river city with flags and a friendly swagger. Less of a tourist shop window than CABA, more human scale and waterfront.

What to do
National Flag Memorial, the costanera, islands across the water, cultural centres. If there is a football derby, the mood shifts.
Eat
The carlito (toasted sandwich of crustless bread, ham, cheese, butter, and ketchup; local heritage — it started in bars like Cachito). Also river fish and ice cream.
How to get there
Bus from Retiro (~3–4 h), the train to Rosario Norte when it is running, or a flight to ROS (~1 h). The most logical short hop from Buenos Aires.
Tours
Walk the city. Island boats leave from the waterfront; ask at the pier and check the river conditions.
Visited by Aldo

Córdoba

Córdoba Province · Sierras

From BA Bus ~9–11 h · often overnight Flight ~1.5 h

University city, cuarteto music, fernet and Coke, hills a short drive away. The city is lively; weekends leak out to Carlos Paz or the valley.

What to do
Historic centre and the Jesuit Block (UNESCO), Sierras Chicas or Calamuchita, Camino de las Altas Cumbres if you drive calmly.
Eat
Fernet and Coke (yes, it is cultural), locro, Córdoba empanadas, asado. In the hills, goat and inn food.
How to get there
Fly to COR (a real domestic hub, ~1 h 20) or bus (~9–11 h) from Retiro. A car pays off if you will be in the sierras for several days.
Tours
Jesuit estancias work as self-guided visits. For the hills, a rental car or a tourist van; no single operator owns the market.
Visited by Aldo

CABA

City of Buenos Aires

From BA You’re here AEP 20–40 min · EZE 45–75 min

The capital is huge and contradictory: cafés, protests, tango, malbec, and an energy that wears you out and wins you over. It is not “Argentina”: it is one layer.

What to do
Palermo (parks, bars), San Telmo (Sunday market), Recoleta (cemetery, museums), Puerto Madero for the photo, La Boca briefly with your bag in front. Subte + walking.
Eat
Parrilla (famous Don Julio in Palermo — book), bodegón (El Preferido de Palermo), pan pizza at Güerrín, historic coffee at Café Tortoni and Los 36 Billares, choripán at markets (La Choripanería in Mercado de San Telmo is a frequently cited classic). Dulce de leche ice cream at any serious heladería.
How to get there
EZE (international) or AEP (domestic and some regional). Tienda León / Uber downtown. Inside the city: SUBE, subway, buses, DiDi/Cabify/Uber.
Tours
Free walking tours at Plaza de Mayo/San Telmo (tip-based). Tango in a real milonga if you are game, not only a dinner show. For the Tigre delta, train + boat. Check current milongas on forums: they move.
Visited by Aldo

Buenos Aires Province

Interior of Buenos Aires Province

From CABA 1–6 h Train / bus / car by town

The province is not only the coast: towns, farmland, Tandil, small hills, and a more almacén scale of Argentina. Mar del Plata has its own card.

What to do
La Plata if you care about architecture and the natural-history museum, Tandil or Sierra de la Ventana for air, weekend estancias. Greater Buenos Aires is a different movie: go with a local.
Eat
Tandil cheese and salami, town bakeries, countryside asado. Less Instagram, more counter.
How to get there
Train, bus, or car from CABA. Map distances look short and then the highway stretches them.
Tours
Book estancias directly. You do not need a tour bus for the nearby interior.
Visited by Aldo

Mar del Plata

Atlantic coast · Buenos Aires Province

From BA Bus ~5–6 h Flight ~1 h

The mass beach resort of Argentines. In summer it is a beautiful mess; off season, harbour and wind.

What to do
The rambla and beaches (Playa Grande, Bristol), the port and sea lions, Barrio Los Troncos, a siesta because the sun is no joke.
Eat
Seafood and milanesa napolitana with geological portions of fries. Fries and churros on the rambla. The port for simpler fish.
How to get there
Bus from Retiro (~5–6 h), train from Constitución when it is running, or a flight to MDQ (~1 h). January buses sell out: book.
Tours
Walk and taxi. Short harbour hops if you want; you do not need an expensive package.
Visited by Aldo

Mendoza

Mendoza Province · Cuyo

From BA Bus ~14–16 h · overnight Flight ~2 h

Wine and mountains. The city is flat and tree-lined; the Andes are the backdrop. If you like malbec, it is a required stop.

What to do
Wineries in Luján de Cuyo, Maipú, or Valle de Uco; Uspallata and high mountain roads; Aconcagua from the park (looking counts; you do not have to climb).
Eat
Asado, Mendoza empanadas, goat, olives. Toast with malbec, torrontés if you just came from the north, or a high-altitude white in Uco.
How to get there
Fly to MDZ (~1 h 50) or bus (~14–16 h). Car or bike in Maipú; for Valle de Uco use a tour or a driver: wine does not drive.
Tours
Trout & Wine is a known small-group wine-tour operator (English/Spanish). You can also book bodegas directly. In Maipú, bike + a cab back is the cheap classic.
Visited by Aldo

Puerto Madryn

Chubut · Atlantic Patagonia

From BA Bus ~18–20 h · overnight Flight ~2 h + 1 h transfer

Cold sea, wind, and wildlife. The town is a base; Península Valdés is the reason.

What to do
Southern right whale watching (June–December) from Puerto Pirámides, penguins in season, elephant seals, Gaiman if you want Welsh tea.
Eat
Lamb, hake, king crab when it is in, Welsh cake in Gaiman. Seafood here is fresher than fancy.
How to get there
Fly to REL (Trelew, ~2 h) + transfer (~1 h) or an overnight bus from Buenos Aires (~18–20 h). A car helps on the peninsula.
Tours
Hydro Sport runs whale watching from Pirámides and dolphin trips from Madryn (seasonal; sales sometimes close). Other boats work the pier: compare, and do not chase the animals. You can also drive Península Valdés or take a land tour.
Visited by Aldo

Bariloche

Río Negro · lake Patagonia

From BA Bus ~20–24 h · overnight Flight ~2 h

Lakes, forest, chocolate, and a town that fills with school leavers in high season. The landscape forgives the rest.

What to do
Circuito Chico, Cerro Catedral (hiking or skiing), a boat on Nahuel Huapi, the Seven Lakes road toward Villa La Angostura if you have a car (Neuquén is another province: I marked Bariloche).
Eat
Chocolate (Rapa Nui at Mitre and Villegas is a shop-window classic), trout, lamb, craft beer. A chocolate-thick tea after a hill is part of the ritual.
How to get there
Fly to BRC (~2 h, lots of frequencies) or a ~20–24 h bus. A car for the loops; in winter, snow chains and patience.
Tours
Circuito Chico works by car, bike, or tourist bus. Cerro Catedral has official mountain lifts. For boats to the arrayanes forest or other islands, buy at the pier and watch the weather. I will not invent a single “best” lake operator: the forecast decides.
Visited by Aldo

El Calafate

Santa Cruz · ice Patagonia

From BA Flight ~3 h Bus: no (2+ days)

A base town for Perito Moreno. The glacier is the reason; the town is wind, souvenirs, and lamb.

What to do
Glacier boardwalks (non-negotiable), a boat along the wall, ice trekking if you are fit. El Chaltén is a separate trip (I have not marked it as visited here).
Eat
Patagonian lamb, calafate berry in jam or ice cream, trout. Mountain-hut portions, not tasting-menu ones.
How to get there
Fly to FTE (~3 h). There is no reasonable direct bus from Buenos Aires: if you do not fly, it is a multi-leg odyssey.
Tours
Hielo y Aventura is the park concessionaire: Safari Náutico, Minitrekking, and other glacier-side trips. The boardwalks are self-guided plus the park ticket. Pack a jacket even in January.
Visited by Aldo

Ushuaia

Tierra del Fuego · the end of the world

From BA Flight ~3 h 30 Overland: 2 days

Mountains dropped into the sea, wind that restyles your hair, and a town that lives on tourism and on being the southernmost place most visitors will ever stand.

What to do
Tierra del Fuego National Park, Beagle Channel (lighthouses, sea lions, cormorants), Laguna Esmeralda, Cerro Martial. Ski in winter; long hikes in summer.
Eat
King crab, black hake, Fuegian lamb. It is pricey next to the north: freight shows up on the bill.
How to get there
Fly to USH (~3 h 30). Overland is two days and a crossing: almost nobody does that for a visit. The airport sits against the town.
Tours
Taun is a local mountain-guide company (trekking, Vinciguerra glacier, packrafting, Laguna Esmeralda). For the Beagle, several boats leave the port: compare length and whether they include the penguin island (seasonal). Check the forecast: wind cancels.

Getting around

Argentine prices move. Really. Inflation, season, the exchange rate, and “two seats left on promo” change everything. Ranges below mix promo fares (JetSMART/Flybondi 2025–early 2026) and more typical high-season prices (press surveys, Jan–Nov 2026). USD at a reference official rate of ~ARS 1,550 (BNA sell ~1,565 on 2 Nov 2026). Do not budget to the peso with this: use it to know whether you are talking about a coffee or a salary. Check Aerolíneas, Flybondi, JetSMART, Plataforma10 or Central de Pasajes on the day you buy.

Argentina is big. Buenos Aires has the subte (subway), colectivos (buses), and relatively cheap taxis/Uber. Between cities, flying usually wins when the bus is 15–24 hours. Long-distance buses are comfortable (semi-cama / cama / suite): not a sad Greyhound, but your body will notice.

How long it actually takes — from Buenos Aires

“Close” in Argentina can still be a full day A Tucumán local will tell you Córdoba is just around the corner: that is a 9–11 h bus. Times are one-way, typical ranges: buses from Retiro, flights from AEP (sometimes EZE). They do not include check-in, the taxi to the airport, or the transfer at the other end. From ~10 h the sane bus is an overnight cama: leave in the afternoon, breakfast in the other terminal.

Domestic air network

Three names: Aerolíneas Argentinas (the fullest network), Flybondi and JetSMART (low-cost; bags and seats cost extra). Useful hubs:

  • AEP (Aeroparque, Jorge Newbery) — most Buenos Aires domestic flights. Closer to the centre than Ezeiza.
  • EZE (Ezeiza) — international; some connecting domestics.
  • COR Córdoba, MDZ Mendoza, BRC Bariloche, USH Ushuaia, plus IGR (Iguazú), SLA (Salta), TUC, FTE (Calafate), REL (Trelew/Madryn), ROS, MDQ, JUJ, SDE.

Low-cost: read the fine print. A “USD 40” fare with no cabin bag looks like a normal fare once you tick the boxes.

Buses: platforms, companies, and Starlink

The hub is Retiro bus terminal in CABA. Bring ID or a passport, arrive ~30 minutes early, and do not marry the first fare you see: aggregators and company sites do not always show the same seats. As-of 2025–2026; pesos move (see the disclaimer above).

Aggregators — they sell tickets; they do not drive the bus

Plataforma10 and Central de Pasajes are marketplaces. People say “central de buses”; the site is Central de Pasajes. Search the same origin/date on both (and sometimes on the company site): price and seat can differ.

PlatformWhat it isPay / ticket
Plataforma10 Founded 2001 in Buenos Aires; now part of Travelier (formerly Bookaway). Compares 300+ carriers and thousands of destinations; web + app; sister sites in Brazil, Paraguay, Peru, and Colombia. E-ticket. Mercado Pago, MODO, installments. National-service refunds typically keep 10% (>48 h), 20% (48–24 h), 30% (within 24 h). Always read checkout; some promos are non-refundable.
Central de Pasajes Long-standing Argentine marketplace (not a terminal). 90+ companies (some listings run to ~130); web + app; agency channel too. E-ticket. MP / MODO / installments. Same SETOP 10/20/30% ballpark on national services, plus ~5% admin. The electronic service fee is often non-refundable.
Busbud / CheckMyBus Optional internationals, handy if you are paying with a foreign card or want the corridor in USD. They do not replace the two local platforms. Varies. Treat amenity lists (WiFi, etc.) with skepticism: the listing may not match that departure’s coach.

Starlink onboard — what companies advertise, not an audit of every coach
  • Advertises Starlink Flecha Bus and the rest of Grupo Flecha (Chevallier, Urquiza, La Veloz del Norte): in 2025 they announced 170+ new coaches with Starlink, PinTV, and live geolocation. Confirm on that departure: not every bus in the brand is a 2025 unit.
  • Advertises Starlink El Cometa Bis: says Starlink WiFi on all micros (cama and semicama).
  • No reliable Starlink claim Tony Tur: do not mark it as Starlink. Busbud often lists entertainment without WiFi (other aggregators conflict). The company sells comfort/TV on the MdP corridor, not satellite internet.
Everyone else: old-school bus WiFi, or none. Even if the coach says Starlink, bring offline maps and downloads. Quality still varies with the roof, the passenger count, and the pampa.

Which is “best”

  • Nationwide versatility → Flecha (compare price vs Andesmar/Chevallier that day)
  • Mendoza / long south / Chile → Andesmar is often strong
  • Bariloche tourist premium → Vía Bariloche; budget: Flecha or Andesmar in Cama
  • BA ↔ Mar del Plata → Tony Tur (comfort/punctuality) vs Plusmar/Rutatlántica (frequency/price)
  • Always: same origin/date on Plataforma10 + Central de Pasajes; Cama overnight; Retiro ~30 min early

Useful apps

  • Cómo Llego — public transit in Buenos Aires
  • BA Móvil / SUBE — to top up the transit card; register it or you pay more
  • Uber / Cabify / DiDi — big cities; from EZE prefer an official remise or the app, not the guy with a cardboard sign

When a rental car makes sense

Yes: Mendoza (wineries), Salta–Jujuy (the quebrada), Bariloche (the loops), Península Valdés, Córdoba’s sierras. No: CABA (traffic, parking, smashed windows). In Patagonia distances are huge and some roads are gravel; hail/wind insurance is the hidden line item. Drive defensively: loose animals, trucks, and “scenic” often means no shoulder.

Money and exchange

Important warning Argentina’s exchange situation changes constantly. What’s here is a general guide, but rates, regulations, and apps can vary. Check current sources before you travel.

Historically, Argentina had an official dollar (the bank/ATM rate) and a blue dollar (informal exchange, a better rate). The gap was huge for years. By late 2026 the official BNA rate and the blue were much closer (both around ARS 1,500–1,570 in early November). Depending on when you read this, political changes may have unified or rewritten the rules again.

Practical advice

  • Bring cash dollars if you can — they’ve always been useful for getting a better rate
  • Ask a local you trust where to exchange, or use apps like Belo / Let'sBit that operate legally
  • International credit cards apply the official rate (and sometimes “dólar tarjeta” surcharges): not always the best
  • Many places accept payment by transfer (Mercado Pago, Modo) — handy if you have a local account
  • Don’t exchange everything at the airport. Find out first.

Food by region

Beyond the national asado: each zone has its own culinary stubbornness. The cards above go local; this is the mental map.

Asado and parrilla

A secular religion. Vacío, tira de asado, chorizo, blood sausage, chinchulines, sweetbreads. If you are invited, go and stay: it is slow and social. In CABA there are shop-window parrillas (Don Julio) and neighborhood ones that feed you better for less.

Empanadas (yes, they change)

  • Salta/Jujuy: juicy, often baked, knife-cut beef
  • Tucumán: another cult; order them with the sandwich
  • Cuyo: sometimes drier / oven-baked, with olive
  • Patagonia: lamb, sometimes hake
  • Humita (corn) in the north

Milanesa and sandwiches

Milanesa napolitana with fries is the default Argentine lunch. In Tucumán the sánguche de milanesa is identity. In Rosario, the carlito. In CABA, the bodegón.

Choripán

Chorizo on bread, chimichurri, salsa criolla. Stadiums, markets, waterfronts. Street food with no costume.

Interior stews

Locro (national holidays, winter), humita and tamales in the northwest, stews, goat. Sunday family food, not a tasting menu.

Facturas and dulce de leche

Medialunas (lard or butter), vigilantes, sacramentos. Dulce de leche ice cream, alfajores. Argentine breakfast is coffee + pastry, not avocado toast.

Wine

Mendoza = malbec and altitude (Uco). Cafayate = torrontés. Patagonia (Río Negro/Neuquén) makes wine too, though I marked Mendoza as the stop. Order a glass, not only a bottle.

Sea and river

Mar del Plata and Madryn: fish and shellfish. Iguazú and Rosario/Entre Ríos: river (surubí, boga). Ushuaia: king crab and black hake, at end-of-the-world prices.

Mate, asado, and cafés

Food is a central part of Argentine culture. Asado is almost a religion — if you’re invited to one, go. It’s slow, social, and there’s a lot of meat. The asador is in charge: don’t poke the meat to death, don’t order a Manhattan steakhouse doneness, and say thank you.

Mate

Mate is the shared infusion par excellence. It’s not just a drink — it’s a social ritual. If they offer you some, accept. Drink what they pass you, say “gracias” when you don’t want more, and hand the mate back. Don’t stir it, don’t say “it’s too hot/cold,” just drink. Don’t touch the bombilla with your hand. Only the cebador moves the yerba.

Cafés and bars

Buenos Aires has a strong café culture, with historic bars (Café Tortoni, Los 36 Billares) and modern spots (specialty coffee in Palermo). Coffee is more European than American — cortado, café con leche, lágrima. Ask for “a coffee” and you will get a look: specify.

Safety and street smarts

Argentina isn’t a dangerous country in general, but as in any big city, you need to stay alert. Phone and wallet thefts happen, especially in tourist areas and on public transit.

Basic tips

  • Don’t take your phone out on the street without paying attention. If you have to use it, do it quickly or somewhere quieter
  • Don’t use open backpacks on the subway or buses — pickpockets exist
  • Be careful at night — some areas are fine (Belgrano, Núñez, Colegiales), others less so. Ask before walking alone late at night
  • Don’t flash cash on the street — pay discreetly, keep your wallet in a front pocket
  • Taxi from the airport — use official remises or Uber, not random taxis that offer you a ride
  • In Iguazú and the north, the more realistic risks are sometimes sun, altitude, or mosquitoes — not only the street corner

That said, most people are kind and helpful. If you’re lost, ask — Argentines usually help with directions (even if they sometimes send you the wrong way with total confidence).

What NOT to do

  • Don’t talk about the Malvinas/Falklands if you don’t want a political argument
  • Don’t compare Argentina with other Latin American countries in a condescending way — Argentines like to set themselves apart (for better or worse)
  • Don’t expect British punctuality — “I’ll be there in 10 minutes” is usually 30. It’s cultural
  • Don’t ask for “a coffee” without specifying — ask what they have. “Café americano” exists but isn’t the most common
  • Don’t automatically leave a 10% tip — check whether the “cubierto” or table service is already included
  • Don’t take offense if they call you “che” or “boludo” — they’re colloquial forms of address, not insults (depends on the tone, of course)
  • Don’t assume the whole country is Buenos Aires. Córdoba, Rosario, and the north will correct you

Best time to visit

It depends on where you’re going. Argentina has opposite climates in different regions:

  • Buenos Aires — spring (Sep–Nov) and fall (Mar–May) are ideal. Summer (Dec–Feb) is hot and humid; winter (Jun–Aug) is cool but not extreme
  • Patagonia — summer (Dec–Mar) for trekking; winter (Jun–Sep) for snow and skiing. Calafate “works” most of the year; Ushuaia in winter is wind and short days
  • The north (Salta, Jujuy, Tucumán) — avoid summer (Dec–Feb) if you don’t like extreme heat. Fall/spring are better. Altitude: a jacket for the night even if noon burns
  • Mendoza — spring and fall are ideal for wineries; winter for skiing at Las Leñas (different logistics)
  • Iguazú — year-round, but it’s tropical — always hot and humid. Spring has a higher water volume
  • Madryn — whales roughly June–December; penguins on another window. Don’t expect a year-round safari
  • Mar del Plata / the coast — the average Argentine goes in January. If you hate crowds, November or March

Remember that the seasons are reversed (Southern Hemisphere) — December is summer, July is winter.

One last tip

Argentines can be intense, direct, and full of opinions (about soccer, politics, asado, anything). But they’re also warm, generous with food and time, and they’ll welcome you well if you come with good vibes.

Argentina has its quilombos — inflation, chaotic politics, bureaucracy — but it also has incredible landscapes, excellent food, and people who will go out of their way to make you feel at home.

Enjoy it. And bring dollars. 😉
