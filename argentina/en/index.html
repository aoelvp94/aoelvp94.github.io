A guide to enjoying Argentina

Practical tips for friends and visitors, from an Argentine who spent years traveling the world and came back to appreciate what’s good about his country. This is not a formal tourist guide — it’s what I’d tell someone I know who’s coming to visit, now with a map, real travel times from Buenos Aires, hack trips, and one page per province I actually went to.

Getting around

Argentine prices move. Really. Inflation, season, the exchange rate, and “two seats left on promo” change everything. Ranges below mix promo fares (JetSMART 2025–2026) and more typical high-season prices (press surveys, Jan–Nov 2026). USD at a reference official rate of ~ARS 1,550 (BNA sell ~1,565 on 2 Nov 2026). Do not budget to the peso with this: use it to know whether you are talking about a coffee or a salary. Check Aerolíneas, JetSMART, Plataforma10 or Central de Pasajes on the day you buy.

Argentina is big. Buenos Aires has the subte (subway), colectivos (buses), and relatively cheap taxis/Uber. Between cities, flying usually wins when the bus is 15–24 hours. Long-distance buses are comfortable (semi-cama / cama / suite): not a sad Greyhound, but your body will notice.

How long it actually takes — from Buenos Aires

“Close” in Argentina can still be a full day A Tucumán local will tell you Córdoba is just around the corner: that is a 9–11 h bus. Times are one-way, typical ranges: buses from Retiro, flights from AEP (sometimes EZE). They do not include check-in, the taxi to the airport, or the transfer at the other end. From ~10 h the sane bus is an overnight cama: leave in the afternoon, breakfast in the other terminal.

Domestic air network

To book as of early November 2026, two names: Aerolíneas Argentinas (the fullest network) and JetSMART (the low-cost that is still flying; bags and seats cost extra). Useful hubs:

  • AEP (Aeroparque, Jorge Newbery) — most Buenos Aires domestic flights. Closer to the centre than Ezeiza.
  • EZE (Ezeiza) — international; some connecting domestics.
  • COR Córdoba, MDZ Mendoza, BRC Bariloche, USH Ushuaia, plus IGR (Iguazú), SLA (Salta), TUC, FTE (Calafate), REL (Trelew/Madryn), ROS, MDQ, JUJ, SDE.

Low-cost: read the fine print. A “USD 40” fare with no cabin bag looks like a normal fare once you tick the boxes. The low-cost map changes fast: a brand that flew last year may not take off tomorrow — do not assume an old logo still sells tickets. Other names show up on a one-off or a charter (and LADE covers a handful of Patagonia public-service routes); do not build an itinerary around them.

Buses: platforms, companies, and Starlink

The hub is Retiro bus terminal in CABA. Bring ID or a passport, arrive ~30 minutes early, and do not marry the first fare you see: aggregators and company sites do not always show the same seats. As-of 2025–2026; pesos move (see the disclaimer above).

Aggregators — they sell tickets; they do not drive the bus

Plataforma10 and Central de Pasajes are marketplaces. People say “central de buses”; the site is Central de Pasajes. Search the same origin/date on both (and sometimes on the company site): price and seat can differ.

PlatformWhat it isPay / ticket
Plataforma10 Founded 2001 in Buenos Aires; now part of Travelier (formerly Bookaway). Compares 300+ carriers and thousands of destinations; web + app; sister sites in Brazil, Paraguay, Peru, and Colombia. E-ticket. Mercado Pago, MODO, installments. National-service refunds typically keep 10% (>48 h), 20% (48–24 h), 30% (within 24 h). Always read checkout; some promos are non-refundable.
Central de Pasajes Long-standing Argentine marketplace (not a terminal). 90+ companies (some listings run to ~130); web + app; agency channel too. E-ticket. MP / MODO / installments. Same SETOP 10/20/30% ballpark on national services, plus ~5% admin. The electronic service fee is often non-refundable.
Busbud / CheckMyBus Optional internationals, handy if you are paying with a foreign card or want the corridor in USD. They do not replace the two local platforms. Varies. Treat amenity lists (WiFi, etc.) with skepticism: the listing may not match that departure’s coach.

Starlink onboard — what companies advertise, not an audit of every coach
  • Advertises Starlink Flecha Bus and the rest of Grupo Flecha (Chevallier, Urquiza, La Veloz del Norte): in 2025 they announced 170+ new coaches with Starlink, PinTV, and live geolocation. Confirm on that departure: not every bus in the brand is a 2025 unit.
  • Advertises Starlink El Cometa Bis: says Starlink WiFi on all micros (cama and semicama).
  • No reliable Starlink claim Tony Tur: do not mark it as Starlink. Busbud often lists entertainment without WiFi (other aggregators conflict). The company sells comfort/TV on the MdP corridor, not satellite internet.
Everyone else: old-school bus WiFi, or none. Even if the coach says Starlink, bring offline maps and downloads. Quality still varies with the roof, the passenger count, and the pampa.

Which is “best”

  • Nationwide versatility → Flecha (compare price vs Andesmar/Chevallier that day)
  • Mendoza / long south / Chile → Andesmar is often strong
  • Bariloche tourist premium → Vía Bariloche; budget: Flecha or Andesmar in Cama
  • BA ↔ Mar del Plata → Tony Tur (comfort/punctuality) vs Plusmar/Rutatlántica (frequency/price)
  • Always: same origin/date on Plataforma10 + Central de Pasajes; Cama overnight; Retiro ~30 min early

When a rental car makes sense

Yes: Mendoza (wineries), Salta–Jujuy (the quebrada), Bariloche (the loops), Península Valdés, Córdoba’s sierras. No: CABA (traffic, parking, smashed windows). In Patagonia distances are huge and some roads are gravel; hail/wind insurance is the hidden line item. Drive defensively: loose animals, trucks, and “scenic” often means no shoulder.

Hack trips

Side-trips and border hops foreigners often miss: Purmamarca→Chile via Jama, Montevideo by bus (not only Buquebus), the other Iguazú park. These are routes people actually run — I am not inventing a ferry. Order-of-magnitude prices, as-of 2025–Nov 2026, ~ARS 1,550/USD; pesos move (see the disclaimer).

Passport, visas, the border Mercosur: an Argentine/Uruguayan/etc. national ID often works between member states. Everyone else: passport. Chile and Uruguay, short tourism — most Western passports enter visa-free; Brazil asks some nationalities for an e-visa (USA, Australia, Canada — check Itamaraty); Bolivia asks Americans for a visa (~USD 160, sometimes at the border) and sometimes a yellow-fever certificate. Your nationality decides: this page is not a consulate. On an international bus, get off at immigration even if the locals stay seated.

Mendoza ↔ Santiago de Chile

Bus ~7–8 h Flight ~1 h Pass: snow/wind

The classic crossing via Paso Cristo Redentor (Los Libertadores). Buses with Andesmar, CATA, El Rápido Internacional: Mendoza terminal → Santiago Terminal Sur. Day and night departures (Andesmar lists ~22:00). The MDZ–SCL flight (~1 h, JetSMART/SKY/LATAM) skips the mountains when the pass is shut.

  • Money — one-way bus from ~ARS 45,000–70,000 (~USD 30–45) by class/date (press, Nov 2026). Promo flights from ~ARS 62,000 (~USD 40); typical fares run higher. Chile is often one hour behind.
  • The pass closes — snow and wind, sometimes for days or weeks (in 2026 Cristo Redentor was closed ~90 days). Buses get rebooked or refunded; it is not a guaranteed shopping hop. Check Gendarmería / Vialidad the day before.

Skip it in deep winter with no flight backup; and as a “same-day shopping tour” if the pass has been unstable. Also skip flying BA→SCL if what you wanted was Mendoza: those are different trips.

Purmamarca / Jujuy → Chile (Paso de Jama)

Purmamarca ~8 h Jujuy ~8 h 45 4,200 m · not 24 h

The real crossing from the Quebrada is not Cristo Redentor: it is Paso de Jama (Argentine RN 52 → Chilean CH-27, ~4,200 m; on the Chilean side the road brushes ~4,800 m before dropping into San Pedro de Atacama). Paved. Andesmar is the steadiest frequency: leaves Salta ~1:00, San Salvador de Jujuy terminal ~2:45, stops in Purmamarca ~3:40, San Pedro ~11:30 (Oct 2026). Chilean Pullman Bus shows up some days from Salta. Buy online or in Jujuy/Salta: Purmamarca is not where you count on an international ticket window.

  • Real time — the timetable says ~8 h; customs, ice, or white-out wind stretch it to 12 h. The pass is not 24 h (2026 reference: ~8–20, shorter in winter). Chile is often one hour behind.
  • Papers and SAG — passport (or Argentine DNI). Chile is strict about fruit, meat, dairy, seeds: eat them first. Puna altitude: hydrate; this is not a day trip from Tilcara.
  • Closures — snow, ice, and wind, not only in winter (Sept/Oct 2026 had shutdowns). Check Gendarmería the day before. In winter Andesmar sometimes cancels or leaves late.
  • Money — order of magnitude ~USD 75–100 one-way on aggregators (2025–26); pesos move, compare Andesmar that day.

Skip it as a day outing from Purmamarca (pre-dawn + border + desert). And skip driving a rented Argentine car into Chile without checking whether the contract allows it. The hop to Bolivia is a different pass (La Quiaca), not Jama.

Iguazú AR ↔ BR (the other park)

1–2 h each way with the border Brazil is a full day

The Argentine side is walkways and Garganta; the Brazilian side is the panoramic postcard. Rio Uruguay / Crucero del Norte buses from Puerto Iguazú terminal (“Cataratas Brasil”), hourly from ~7:30. Passport. Ask the driver to stop at Brazilian immigration: if you are not stamped, you are in irregularly. The bus sometimes does not wait — keep the ticket for the next one.

Skip cramming both parks into one day. If you have only one, stay on the Argentine side. Brazil: a visa if your passport needs one (US and others: e-visa).

Buenos Aires → Montevideo by bus

EGA ~8 h 30 overnight Ferry+bus ~3 h 30

There is a land bus, not only the boat. EGA (Empresa General Artigas) leaves Retiro ~22:00 and reaches Tres Cruces ~6:30 (~8 h 30, semicama/cama). It crosses the Libertador General San Martín Bridge toward Fray Bentos. From ~ARS 78,000 / ~USD 50 one-way (Oct 2026, Plataforma10). Passport or Mercosur ID. If you are already in Entre Ríos, the same corridor hits Gualeguaychú / Fray Bentos.

  • Vs ferry — Colonia Express to Montevideo is boat to Colonia + bus (~3 h 30, from ~ARS 119,000). Buquebus direct to Montevideo port ~2 h 30, often USD 160+ one-way. Colonia alone (day trip): ferry ~1 h 15, from ~ARS 64,000 / ~USD 40–55 (Outlet is non-refundable).
  • The bus wins on price and on “leave at night, breakfast in Tres Cruces.” The boat wins if you value the daytime and the duty-free.

Skip Montevideo “in a day” by bus (you arrive in the morning; you are not back that night). Colonia does fit a ferry day. Tigre is not Uruguay: no passport.

CABA day-trips (no other country)

Tigre ~50 min La Plata ~1 h 15

Mitre line, Tigre branch, from Retiro (~ARS 790 with a registered SUBE, Oct 2026): Puerto de Frutos, a delta boat, back for dinner. La Plata: Roca line from Constitución (~1 h 16). San Antonio de Areco: ~2 h bus from Retiro if you want gaucho country.

Skip Tandil as a day trip (~5 h bus): that is a weekend. And skip a paid “city tour” to Tigre when the train leaves all the time.

Northwest: Salta–Jujuy loop (and Bolivia)

Salta↔La Quiaca ~7 h Quebrada by car/tour

The solid combo is Salta + Quebrada de Humahuaca + Salinas, 4–5 days, car or tour. If you continue to Bolivia: bus Salta→La Quiaca (~7 h, Andesmar/Balut, from ~ARS 35,000 / ~USD 23, Oct 2026), walk the bridge into Villazón, then Tupiza/Uyuni/La Paz. There is another crossing (Bermejo–Aguas Blancas) more for people coming from Tarija.

Skip Salta→La Paz in one sitting (36–48 h of pure road). Bolivian visa if your nationality needs one. The hop to Chile from the Quebrada is Jama (card above), not this bridge.

Puerto Madryn + Península Valdés

Pirámides ~1 h 15 Whales Jun–Dec

Madryn is the base; Valdés is the reason. Puerto Pirámides is ~95 km. A car (the sane option) or the Mar y Valle bus. Southern right whales: June to December (2026 season from 9 Jun); El Doradillo, from shore, Jun–Oct. Protected-area entry 2026: ~ARS 20,000 residents / ~ARS 50,000 foreigners (~USD 13–32). The boat trip is extra, and the harbour closes in wind.

Skip going in January “for whales” (they are gone). And skip trying to cover the whole peninsula in a morning without a car: the wildlife is spread out.

El Calafate + El Chaltén

~2 h 40 · 215 km Chaltén: I did not mark it

The glacier is Calafate; the trekking is Chaltén. Daily buses (Cal Tur, Chaltén Travel, TAQSA), ~ARS 70,000 / ~USD 45 one-way (Oct 2026) plus a terminal fee. Book 2–3 nights in Chaltén after Perito Moreno. El Chaltén has no airport: you arrive overland.

Skip it as a trekking day trip from Calafate (the bus eats the daylight). And skip skipping the glacier to go straight to Chaltén if this is your only time on the ice.

Bariloche ↔ Chilean lakes

Samoré 6–8 h Cruce Andino ~12 h

Two different products. The bus via Paso Cardenal Samoré to Osorno / Puerto Montt / Puerto Varas (Andesmar and others) is transport: 6–8 h, ~USD 25–45. The Cruce Andino is the three-lake outing (Nahuel Huapi, Frías, Todos los Santos): ~12 h, USD 355 non-resident / 277 resident (2026–28 fare); parks and lunch extra. It runs almost every day; the lake weather decides.

Skip paying Cruce Andino prices for a transfer. And Villa La Angostura is not Chile: it is Neuquén, and I did not mark it.

Ushuaia ↔ Punta Arenas

~11–12 h + ferry No direct ferry

A real route, not a day trip: Bus-Sur / Tecni Austral, two borders and a ~30 min ferry across the Strait of Magellan (Punta Delgada). Not every day; in summer it sells out weeks ahead. ~USD 40–70 / CLP 58–68k one-way (2026 catalogue). Worth it if you continue to Puerto Natales / Torres del Paine. There is no useful short flight: the plane goes the long way via SCL/BA.

Skip it if you have two days in Ushuaia (you lose one on the bus). And do not plan on a “direct Ushuaia–Punta Arenas ferry”: it does not exist, except multi-day cruises.

Rosario and Córdoba as hubs

ROS ~3–4 h from BA COR ~9–11 h / 1.5 h flight

Rosario is the short hop that still feels like a city: waterfront, carlito, a logical stop toward the litoral. Córdoba is the interior’s air hub and the door to the sierras (Carlos Paz, Calamuchita) — an overnight cama from Retiro or a short flight. From COR you connect to Mendoza and the northwest without bouncing back through Ezeiza.

Skip sleeping in Rosario “because it is on the way” if your flight is BA–Iguazú: you save nothing. And skip Córdoba on foot if what you wanted was the sierras: rent a car or take a combi.

Posadas ↔ Encarnación (Paraguay)

Minutes, not hours San Roque González Bridge

If you are already in Misiones (and not only at Iguazú), Encarnación is across the river: an urban hop, chipá, another currency. Bus or taxi over the international bridge; passport or Mercosur ID. It does not replace the falls, and you do not need a “tour.”

Skip detouring from Puerto Iguazú “just to say you stepped into Paraguay”: Ciudad del Este is a different crossing, more mall than town, and it eats the park day.

Money and exchange

Important warning Argentina’s exchange situation changes constantly. What’s here is a general guide, but rates, regulations, and apps can vary. Check current sources before you travel.

Historically, Argentina had an official dollar (the bank/ATM rate) and a blue dollar (informal exchange, a better rate). The gap was huge for years. By late 2026 the official BNA rate and the blue were much closer (both around ARS 1,500–1,570 in early November). Depending on when you read this, political changes may have unified or rewritten the rules again.

Practical advice

  • Bring cash dollars if you can — they’ve always been useful for getting a better rate
  • Ask a local you trust where to exchange, or use apps like Belo / Let'sBit that operate legally
  • International credit cards apply the official rate (and sometimes “dólar tarjeta” surcharges): not always the best
  • Many places accept payment by transfer (Mercado Pago, Modo) — handy if you have a local account
  • Don’t exchange everything at the airport. Find out first.

Food by region

Beyond the national asado: each zone has its own culinary stubbornness. The cards above go local; this is the mental map.

Asado and parrilla

A secular religion. Vacío, tira de asado, chorizo, blood sausage, chinchulines, sweetbreads. If you are invited, go and stay: it is slow and social. In CABA there are shop-window parrillas (Don Julio) and neighborhood ones that feed you better for less.

Empanadas (yes, they change)

  • Salta/Jujuy: juicy, often baked, knife-cut beef
  • Tucumán: another cult; order them with the sandwich
  • Cuyo: sometimes drier / oven-baked, with olive
  • Patagonia: lamb, sometimes hake
  • Humita (corn) in the north

Milanesa and sandwiches

Milanesa napolitana with fries is the default Argentine lunch. In Tucumán the sánguche de milanesa is identity. In Rosario, the carlito. In CABA, the bodegón.

Choripán

Chorizo on bread, chimichurri, salsa criolla. Stadiums, markets, waterfronts. Street food with no costume.

Interior stews

Locro (national holidays, winter), humita and tamales in the northwest, stews, goat. Sunday family food, not a tasting menu.

Facturas and dulce de leche

Medialunas (lard or butter), vigilantes, sacramentos. Dulce de leche ice cream, alfajores. Argentine breakfast is coffee + pastry, not avocado toast.

Wine

Mendoza = malbec and altitude (Uco). Cafayate = torrontés. Patagonia (Río Negro/Neuquén) makes wine too, though I marked Mendoza as the stop. Order a glass, not only a bottle.

Sea and river

Mar del Plata and Madryn: fish and shellfish. Iguazú and Rosario/Entre Ríos: river (surubí, boga). Ushuaia: king crab and black hake, at end-of-the-world prices.

Mate, asado, and cafés

Food is a central part of Argentine culture. Asado is almost a religion — if you’re invited to one, go. It’s slow, social, and there’s a lot of meat. The asador is in charge: don’t poke the meat to death, don’t order a Manhattan steakhouse doneness, and say thank you.

Mate

Mate is the shared infusion par excellence. It’s not just a drink — it’s a social ritual. If they offer you some, accept. Drink what they pass you, say “gracias” when you don’t want more, and hand the mate back. Don’t stir it, don’t say “it’s too hot/cold,” just drink. Don’t touch the bombilla with your hand. Only the cebador moves the yerba.

Cafés and bars

Buenos Aires has a strong café culture, with historic bars (Café Tortoni, Los 36 Billares) and modern spots (specialty coffee in Palermo). Coffee is more European than American — cortado, café con leche, lágrima. Ask for “a coffee” and you will get a look: specify.

Safety and street smarts

Argentina isn’t a dangerous country in general, but as in any big city, you need to stay alert. Phone and wallet thefts happen, especially in tourist areas and on public transit.

Basic tips

  • Don’t take your phone out on the street without paying attention. If you have to use it, do it quickly or somewhere quieter
  • Don’t use open backpacks on the subway or buses — pickpockets exist
  • Be careful at night — some areas are fine (Belgrano, Núñez, Colegiales), others less so. Ask before walking alone late at night
  • Don’t flash cash on the street — pay discreetly, keep your wallet in a front pocket
  • Taxi from the airport — use official remises or Uber, not random taxis that offer you a ride
  • In Iguazú and the north, the more realistic risks are sometimes sun, altitude, or mosquitoes — not only the street corner

That said, most people are kind and helpful. If you’re lost, ask — Argentines usually help with directions (even if they sometimes send you the wrong way with total confidence).

What NOT to do

  • Don’t talk about the Malvinas/Falklands if you don’t want a political argument
  • Don’t compare Argentina with other Latin American countries in a condescending way — Argentines like to set themselves apart (for better or worse)
  • Don’t expect British punctuality — “I’ll be there in 10 minutes” is usually 30. It’s cultural
  • Don’t ask for “a coffee” without specifying — ask what they have. “Café americano” exists but isn’t the most common
  • Don’t automatically leave a 10% tip — check whether the “cubierto” or table service is already included
  • Don’t take offense if they call you “che” or “boludo” — they’re colloquial forms of address, not insults (depends on the tone, of course)
  • Don’t assume the whole country is Buenos Aires. Córdoba, Rosario, and the north will correct you

Best time to visit

Southern Hemisphere: the seasons are reversed December is summer, July is winter. If you are coming from the north, do not pack as if Christmas meant snow: here it is heat, beach, and asado. One trip will not hit “the perfect month” for both Iguazú and Ushuaia — pick a region.

It depends on where you’re going. One line per place I actually went to: ideal months and what to avoid.

AMBA and the coast

  • CABA / Buenos Aires — Ideal Sep–Nov and Mar–May. Avoid Dec–Feb if you don’t like humid heat and storms; Jun–Aug is cool and grey, not extreme.
  • Buenos Aires (province) — Tigre almost year-round; Tandil and the small sierras better Oct–Nov and Mar–Apr. Avoid January if the plan is the coast road: RN2 fills up.
  • Mar del Plata — Ideal Nov or Mar: beach without the crush. Avoid January (the average Argentine goes then; book the bus). Winter: harbour and wind, many hotels shuttered.

Centre, Cuyo, and the Paraná

  • Rosario — Ideal Sep–Nov and Mar–May, walkable waterfront. Avoid Dec–Feb if you don’t like humid river heat.
  • Córdoba — The city works almost year-round; the sierras are better Sep–Nov and Mar–May. Avoid January in Carlos Paz (heat + crowds). Winter is dry in town, cold at night in the hills.
  • Mendoza — Ideal Oct–Nov and Mar–Apr (wineries; harvest festival around March). Avoid Dec–Feb if you don’t like 35–40 °C. Winter = skiing at Las Leñas/Penitentes: different logistics, not the wine plan.
  • Entre Ríos — Hot springs year-round. Ideal Sep–Nov and Mar–May for the river without the steam. Gualeguaychú carnival: Jan–Feb, that’s the point. Avoid January if you want quiet (and hate mosquitoes).

The north

  • Santiago del Estero — Ideal May–Sep: the heat eases and the hot springs pay off. Avoid Dec–Feb — one of the hottest provinces in the country (40 °C+ and a mandatory siesta).
  • Tucumán — Ideal Apr–Jun and Aug–Oct. Avoid Dec–Feb: humid heat and afternoon storms. Tafí del Valle is cooler year-round.
  • Salta — Ideal Apr–Jun and Sep–Nov (dry, clear days). Avoid Dec–Mar if you don’t want the wet season and valley heat; Cafayate in summer hits hard.
  • Jujuy — Ideal May–Oct in the Quebrada: clear skies, freezing nights, altitude. Avoid Dec–Mar if you want dry roads (rain, occasional closures). A jacket after 6 p.m. is not optional.

Misiones

  • Misiones / Iguazú — Year-round (the jungle is always humid). Ideal Apr–Jun and Aug–Oct: less of an oven, solid water volume. Don’t skip it “because it’s winter”: the park is open. Dec–Feb is hottest, with more storms.

Patagonia

  • Puerto Madryn — Southern right whales Jun–Dec (peak Sep–Oct). Penguins more like Sep–Mar. Don’t plan a Feb–May safari expecting whales: they aren’t there.
  • Bariloche — Lakes/trekking Dec–Mar (best Jan–Mar; Nov–Dec is school-leavers). Ski Jun–Sep. Don’t expect both in one trip. Autumn (Apr–May) is quieter and wetter.
  • El Calafate — Perito Moreno “works” most of the year. Ideal Nov–Mar for daylight (and Chaltén nearby). Avoid May–Aug if you want long treks: cold, short days, some outings get cut. January is expensive and packed.
  • Ushuaia — Trekking and the Beagle: Dec–Mar. Ski: Jun–Aug. Avoid Jun–Jul if the idea is a trail: wind and ~7 h of light. A jacket, every month.

One last tip

Argentines can be intense, direct, and full of opinions (about soccer, politics, asado, anything). But they’re also warm, generous with food and time, and they’ll welcome you well if you come with good vibes.

Argentina has its quilombos — inflation, chaotic politics, bureaucracy — but it also has incredible landscapes, excellent food, and people who will go out of their way to make you feel at home.

Enjoy it. And bring dollars. 😉
