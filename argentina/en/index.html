A guide to enjoying Argentina

Practical tips for friends and visitors, from an Argentine who spent years traveling the world and came back to appreciate what’s good about his country. This is not a formal tourist guide — it’s what I’d tell someone I know who’s coming to visit, now with a map, real travel times from Buenos Aires, hack trips, and one page per province I actually went to.

Getting around

Argentine prices move. Really. Inflation, season, the exchange rate, and “two seats left on promo” change everything. Ranges below mix promo fares (JetSMART 2025–2026) and more typical high-season prices (press surveys, Jan–Nov 2026). USD at a reference official rate of ~ARS 1,550 (BNA sell ~1,565 on 2 Nov 2026). Do not budget to the peso with this: use it to know whether you are talking about a coffee or a salary. Check Aerolíneas, JetSMART, Plataforma10 or Central de Pasajes on the day you buy.

Argentina is big. Buenos Aires has the subte (subway), colectivos (buses), and relatively cheap taxis/Uber. Between cities, flying usually wins when the bus is 15–24 hours. Long-distance buses are comfortable (semi-cama / cama / suite): not a sad Greyhound, but your body will notice.

How long it actually takes — from Buenos Aires

“Close” in Argentina can still be a full day A Tucumán local will tell you Córdoba is just around the corner: that is a 9–11 h bus. Times are one-way, typical ranges: buses from Retiro, flights from AEP (sometimes EZE). They do not include check-in, the taxi to the airport, or the transfer at the other end. From ~10 h the sane bus is an overnight cama: leave in the afternoon, breakfast in the other terminal.

Domestic air network

To book as of early November 2026, two names: Aerolíneas Argentinas (the fullest network) and JetSMART (the low-cost that is still flying; bags and seats cost extra). Useful hubs:

  • AEP (Aeroparque, Jorge Newbery) — most Buenos Aires domestic flights. Closer to the centre than Ezeiza.
  • EZE (Ezeiza) — international; some connecting domestics.
  • COR Córdoba, MDZ Mendoza, BRC Bariloche, USH Ushuaia, plus IGR (Iguazú), SLA (Salta), TUC, CTC (Catamarca), FTE (Calafate), REL (Trelew/Madryn), ROS, MDQ, JUJ, SDE.

Low-cost: read the fine print. A “USD 40” fare with no cabin bag looks like a normal fare once you tick the boxes. The low-cost map changes fast: a brand that flew last year may not take off tomorrow — do not assume an old logo still sells tickets. Other names show up on a one-off or a charter (and LADE covers a handful of Patagonia public-service routes); do not build an itinerary around them.

Buses: platforms, companies, and Starlink

The hub is Retiro bus terminal in CABA. Bring ID or a passport, arrive ~30 minutes early, and do not marry the first fare you see: aggregators and company sites do not always show the same seats. As-of 2025–2026; pesos move (see the disclaimer above).

Aggregators — they sell tickets; they do not drive the bus

Plataforma10 and Central de Pasajes are marketplaces. People say “central de buses”; the site is Central de Pasajes. Search the same origin/date on both (and sometimes on the company site): price and seat can differ.

PlatformWhat it isPay / ticket
Plataforma10 Founded 2001 in Buenos Aires; now part of Travelier (formerly Bookaway). Compares 300+ carriers and thousands of destinations; web + app; sister sites in Brazil, Paraguay, Peru, and Colombia. E-ticket. Mercado Pago, MODO, installments. Starlink onboard — what companies advertise, not an audit of every coach
  • Advertises Starlink Flecha Bus and the rest of Grupo Flecha (Chevallier, Urquiza, La Veloz del Norte): in 2025 they announced 170+ new coaches with Starlink, PinTV, and live geolocation. Confirm on that departure: not every bus in the brand is a 2025 unit.
  • Advertises Starlink El Cometa Bis: says Starlink WiFi on all micros (cama and semicama).
  • No reliable Starlink claim Tony Tur: do not mark it as Starlink. Busbud often lists entertainment without WiFi (other aggregators conflict). The company sells comfort/TV on the MdP corridor, not satellite internet.
Everyone else: old-school bus WiFi, or none. Even if the coach says Starlink, bring offline maps and downloads. Quality still varies with the roof, the passenger count, and the pampa.

When a rental car makes sense

Yes: Mendoza (wineries), Salta–Jujuy (the quebrada), Bariloche (the loops), Península Valdés, Córdoba’s sierras. No: CABA (traffic, parking, smashed windows). In Patagonia distances are huge and some roads are gravel; hail/wind insurance is the hidden line item. Drive defensively: loose animals, trucks, and “scenic” often means no shoulder.

Hack trips

Side-trips and border hops foreigners often miss: Purmamarca→Chile via Jama, Montevideo by bus (not only Buquebus), the other Iguazú park. These are routes people actually run — I am not inventing a ferry. Order-of-magnitude prices, as-of 2025–Nov 2026, ~ARS 1,550/USD; pesos move (see the disclaimer).

Passport, visas, the border Mercosur: an Argentine/Uruguayan/etc. national ID often works between member states. Everyone else: passport. Chile and Uruguay, short tourism — most Western passports enter visa-free; Brazil asks some nationalities for an e-visa (USA, Australia, Canada — check Itamaraty); Bolivia asks Americans for a visa (~USD 160, sometimes at the border) and sometimes a yellow-fever certificate. Your nationality decides: this page is not a consulate. On an international bus, get off at immigration even if the locals stay seated.

Mendoza ↔ Santiago de Chile

Bus ~7–8 h Flight ~1 h Pass: snow/wind

The classic crossing via Paso Cristo Redentor (Los Libertadores). Buses with Andesmar, CATA, El Rápido Internacional: Mendoza terminal → Santiago Terminal Sur. Day and night departures (Andesmar lists ~22:00). The MDZ–SCL flight (~1 h, JetSMART/SKY/LATAM) skips the mountains when the pass is shut.

  • Money — one-way bus from ~ARS 45,000–70,000 (~USD 30–45) by class/date (press, Nov 2026). Promo flights from ~ARS 62,000 (~USD 40); typical fares run higher. Chile is often one hour behind.
  • The pass closes — snow and wind, sometimes for days or weeks (in 2026 Cristo Redentor was closed ~90 days). Buses get rebooked or refunded; it is not a guaranteed shopping hop. Check Gendarmería / Vialidad the day before.

Skip it in deep winter with no flight backup; and as a “same-day shopping tour” if the pass has been unstable. Also skip flying BA→SCL if what you wanted was Mendoza: those are different trips.

Purmamarca / Jujuy → Chile (Paso de Jama)

Purmamarca ~8 h Jujuy ~8 h 45 4,200 m · not 24 h

The real crossing from the Quebrada is not Cristo Redentor: it is Paso de Jama (Argentine RN 52 → Chilean CH-27, ~4,200 m; on the Chilean side the road brushes ~4,800 m before dropping into San Pedro de Atacama). Paved. Andesmar is the steadiest frequency: leaves Salta ~1:00, San Salvador de Jujuy terminal ~2:45, stops in Purmamarca ~3:40, San Pedro ~11:30 (Oct 2026). Chilean Pullman Bus shows up some days from Salta. Buy online or in Jujuy/Salta: Purmamarca is not where you count on an international ticket window.

  • Real time — the timetable says ~8 h; customs, ice, or white-out wind stretch it to 12 h. The pass is not 24 h (2026 reference: ~8–20, shorter in winter). Chile is often one hour behind.
  • Papers and SAG — passport (or Argentine DNI). Chile is strict about fruit, meat, dairy, seeds: eat them first. Puna altitude: hydrate; this is not a day trip from Tilcara.
  • Closures — snow, ice, and wind, not only in winter (Sept/Oct 2026 had shutdowns). Check Gendarmería the day before. In winter Andesmar sometimes cancels or leaves late.
  • Money — order of magnitude ~USD 75–100 one-way on aggregators (2025–26); pesos move, compare Andesmar that day.

Skip it as a day outing from Purmamarca (pre-dawn + border + desert). And skip driving a rented Argentine car into Chile without checking whether the contract allows it. The hop to Bolivia is a different pass (La Quiaca), not Jama.

Iguazú AR ↔ BR (the other park)

1–2 h each way with the border Brazil is a full day

The Argentine side is walkways and Garganta; the Brazilian side is the panoramic postcard. Rio Uruguay / Crucero del Norte buses from Puerto Iguazú terminal (“Cataratas Brasil”), hourly from ~7:30. Passport. Ask the driver to stop at Brazilian immigration: if you are not stamped, you are in irregularly. The bus sometimes does not wait — keep the ticket for the next one.

Skip cramming both parks into one day. If you have only one, stay on the Argentine side. Brazil: a visa if your passport needs one (US and others: e-visa).

Buenos Aires → Montevideo by bus

EGA ~8 h 30 overnight Ferry+bus ~3 h 30

There is a land bus, not only the boat. EGA (Empresa General Artigas) leaves Retiro ~22:00 and reaches Tres Cruces ~6:30 (~8 h 30, semicama/cama). It crosses the Libertador General San Martín Bridge toward Fray Bentos. From ~ARS 78,000 / ~USD 50 one-way (Oct 2026, Plataforma10). Passport or Mercosur ID. If you are already in Entre Ríos, the same corridor hits Gualeguaychú / Fray Bentos.

  • Vs ferry — Colonia Express to Montevideo is boat to Colonia + bus (~3 h 30, from ~ARS 119,000). Buquebus direct to Montevideo port ~2 h 30, often USD 160+ one-way. Colonia alone (day trip): ferry ~1 h 15, from ~ARS 64,000 / ~USD 40–55 (Outlet is non-refundable).
  • The bus wins on price and on “leave at night, breakfast in Tres Cruces.” The boat wins if you value the daytime and the duty-free.

Skip Montevideo “in a day” by bus (you arrive in the morning; you are not back that night). Colonia does fit a ferry day. Tigre is not Uruguay: no passport.

CABA day-trips (no other country)

Tigre ~50 min La Plata ~1 h 15

Mitre line, Tigre branch, from Retiro (~ARS 790 with a registered SUBE, Oct 2026): Puerto de Frutos, a delta boat, back for dinner. La Plata: Roca line from Constitución (~1 h 16). San Antonio de Areco: ~2 h bus from Retiro if you want gaucho country.

Skip Tandil as a day trip (~5 h bus): that is a weekend. And skip a paid “city tour” to Tigre when the train leaves all the time.

Northwest: Salta–Jujuy loop (and Bolivia)

Salta↔La Quiaca ~7 h Quebrada by car/tour

The solid combo is Salta + Quebrada de Humahuaca + Salinas, 4–5 days, car or tour. If you continue to Bolivia: bus Salta→La Quiaca (~7 h, Andesmar/Balut, from ~ARS 35,000 / ~USD 23, Oct 2026), walk the bridge into Villazón, then Tupiza/Uyuni/La Paz. There is another crossing (Bermejo–Aguas Blancas) more for people coming from Tarija.

Skip Salta→La Paz in one sitting (36–48 h of pure road). Bolivian visa if your nationality needs one. The hop to Chile from the Quebrada is Jama (card above), not this bridge.

Puerto Madryn + Península Valdés

Pirámides ~1 h 15 Whales Jun–Dec

Madryn is the base; Valdés is the reason. Puerto Pirámides is ~95 km. A car (the sane option) or the Mar y Valle bus. Southern right whales: June to December (2026 season from 9 Jun); El Doradillo, from shore, Jun–Oct. Protected-area entry 2026: ~ARS 20,000 residents / ~ARS 50,000 foreigners (~USD 13–32). The boat trip is extra, and the harbour closes in wind. Operators I named (Darwin Expediciones, Madrynbuceo) and local peers: card on Chubut — recommendations and public info, not paid ads.

Skip going in January “for whales” (they are gone). And skip trying to cover the whole peninsula in a morning without a car: the wildlife is spread out.

El Calafate + El Chaltén

~2 h 40 · 215 km Chaltén: I did not mark it

The glacier is Calafate; the trekking is Chaltén. Daily buses (Cal Tur, Chaltén Travel, TAQSA), ~ARS 70,000 / ~USD 45 one-way (Oct 2026) plus a terminal fee. Book 2–3 nights in Chaltén after Perito Moreno. El Chaltén has no airport: you arrive overland. In Calafate, kayak or ice — not both the same day. Kayak: Patagonia Chic (free cancel ~24 h on their public policy; verify live). Glacier bus ~ARS 56,000–60,000 one way (Plataforma10, 3 Nov 2026) plus the park.

Skip it as a trekking day trip from Calafate (the bus eats the daylight). And skip skipping the glacier to go straight to Chaltén if this is your only time on the ice.

Bariloche ↔ Chilean lakes

Samoré 6–8 h Cruce Andino ~12 h

Two different products. The bus via Paso Cardenal Samoré to Osorno / Puerto Montt / Puerto Varas (Andesmar and others) is transport: 6–8 h, ~USD 25–45. The Cruce Andino is the three-lake outing (Nahuel Huapi, Frías, Todos los Santos): ~12 h, USD 355 non-resident / 277 resident (2026–28 fare); parks and lunch extra. It runs almost every day; the lake weather decides.

Skip paying Cruce Andino prices for a transfer. And Villa La Angostura is not Chile: it is Neuquén, and I did not mark it.

Ushuaia ↔ Punta Arenas

~11–12 h + ferry No direct ferry

A real route, not a day trip: Bus-Sur / Tecni Austral, two borders and a ~30 min ferry across the Strait of Magellan (Punta Delgada). Not every day; in summer it sells out weeks ahead. ~USD 40–70 / CLP 58–68k one-way (2026 catalogue). Worth it if you continue to Puerto Natales / Torres del Paine. There is no useful short flight: the plane goes the long way via SCL/BA.

Skip it if you have two days in Ushuaia (you lose one on the bus). And do not plan on a “direct Ushuaia–Punta Arenas ferry”: it does not exist, except multi-day cruises.

Rosario and Córdoba as hubs

ROS ~3–4 h from BA COR ~9–11 h / 1.5 h flight

Rosario is the short hop that still feels like a city: waterfront, carlito, a logical stop toward the litoral. Córdoba is the interior’s air hub and the door to the sierras (Carlos Paz, Calamuchita) — an overnight cama from Retiro or a short flight. From COR you connect to Mendoza and the northwest without bouncing back through Ezeiza.

Skip sleeping in Rosario “because it is on the way” if your flight is BA–Iguazú: you save nothing. And skip Córdoba on foot if what you wanted was the sierras: rent a car or take a combi.

Posadas ↔ Encarnación (Paraguay)

Minutes, not hours San Roque González Bridge

If you are already in Misiones (and not only at Iguazú), Encarnación is across the river: an urban hop, chipá, another currency. Bus or taxi over the international bridge; passport or Mercosur ID. It does not replace the falls, and you do not need a “tour.”

Skip detouring from Puerto Iguazú “just to say you stepped into Paraguay”: Ciudad del Este is a different crossing, more mall than town, and it eats the park day.

Money and exchange

Important warning Argentina’s exchange situation changes constantly. What’s here is a general guide, but rates, regulations, and apps can vary. Check current sources before you travel.

Historically, Argentina had an official dollar (the bank/ATM rate) and a blue dollar (informal exchange, a better rate). The gap was huge for years. By late 2026 the official BNA rate and the blue were much closer (both around ARS 1,500–1,570 in early November). Depending on when you read this, political changes may have unified or rewritten the rules again.

Practical advice

  • Bring cash dollars if you can — they’ve always been useful for getting a better rate
  • Ask a local you trust where to exchange, or use apps like Belo / Let'sBit that operate legally
  • International credit cards apply the official rate (and sometimes “dólar tarjeta” surcharges): not always the best
  • Many places accept payment by transfer (Mercado Pago, Modo) — handy if you have a local account
  • Don’t exchange everything at the airport. Find out first.

Food by region

Beyond the national asado: each zone has its own culinary stubbornness. The cards above go local; this is the mental map.

Asado and parrilla

A secular religion. Vacío, tira de asado, chorizo, blood sausage, chinchulines, sweetbreads. If you are invited, go and stay: it is slow and social. In CABA there are shop-window parrillas (Don Julio) and neighborhood ones that feed you better for less.

Empanadas (yes, they change)

  • Salta/Jujuy: juicy, often baked, knife-cut beef
  • Tucumán: another cult; order them with the sandwich
  • Cuyo: sometimes drier / oven-baked, with olive
  • Patagonia: lamb, sometimes hake
  • Humita (corn) in the north

Milanesa and sandwiches

Milanesa napolitana with fries is the default Argentine lunch. In Tucumán the sánguche de milanesa is identity. In Rosario, the carlito. In CABA, the bodegón.

Choripán

Chorizo on bread, chimichurri, salsa criolla. Stadiums, markets, waterfronts. Street food with no costume.

Interior stews

Locro (national holidays, winter), humita and tamales in the northwest, stews, goat. Sunday family food, not a tasting menu.

Facturas and dulce de leche

Medialunas (lard or butter), vigilantes, sacramentos. Dulce de leche ice cream, alfajores. Argentine breakfast is coffee + pastry, not avocado toast.

Wine

Mendoza = malbec and altitude (Uco). Cafayate = torrontés. Patagonia (Río Negro/Neuquén) makes wine too, though I marked Mendoza as the stop. Order a glass, not only a bottle.

Sea and river

Mar del Plata and Madryn: fish and shellfish. Iguazú and Rosario/Entre Ríos: river (surubí, boga). Ushuaia: king crab and black hake, at end-of-the-world prices.

Mate, asado, and cafés

Food is a central part of Argentine culture. Asado is almost a religion — if you’re invited to one, go. It’s slow, social, and there’s a lot of meat. The asador is in charge: don’t poke the meat to death, don’t order a Manhattan steakhouse doneness, and say thank you.

Mate

Mate is the shared infusion par excellence. It’s not just a drink — it’s a social ritual. If they offer you some, accept. Drink what they pass you, say “gracias” when you don’t want more, and hand the mate back. Don’t stir it, don’t say “it’s too hot/cold,” just drink. Don’t touch the bombilla with your hand. Only the cebador moves the yerba.

Cafés and bars

Buenos Aires has a strong café culture, with historic bars (Café Tortoni, Los 36 Billares) and modern spots (specialty coffee in Palermo). Coffee is more European than American — cortado, café con leche, lágrima. Ask for “a coffee” and you will get a look: specify.

Safety and street smarts

Argentina isn’t a dangerous country in general, but as in any big city, you need to stay alert. Phone and wallet thefts happen, especially in tourist areas and on public transit.

Basic tips

  • Don’t take your phone out on the street without paying attention. If you have to use it, do it quickly or somewhere quieter
  • Don’t use open backpacks on the subway or buses — pickpockets exist
  • Be careful at night — some areas are fine (Belgrano, Núñez, Colegiales), others less so. Ask before walking alone late at night
  • Don’t flash cash on the street — pay discreetly, keep your wallet in a front pocket
  • Taxi from the airport — use official remises or Uber, not random taxis that offer you a ride
  • In Iguazú and the north, the more realistic risks are sometimes sun, altitude, or mosquitoes — not only the street corner

That said, most people are kind and helpful. If you’re lost, ask — Argentines usually help with directions (even if they sometimes send you the wrong way with total confidence).

What NOT to do

  • Don’t talk about the Malvinas/Falklands if you don’t want a political argument
  • Don’t compare Argentina with other Latin American countries in a condescending way — Argentines like to set themselves apart (for better or worse)
  • Don’t expect British punctuality — “I’ll be there in 10 minutes” is usually 30. It’s cultural
  • Don’t ask for “a coffee” without specifying — ask what they have. “Café americano” exists but isn’t the most common
  • Don’t automatically leave a 10% tip — check whether the “cubierto” or table service is already included
  • Don’t take offense if they call you “che” or “boludo” — they’re colloquial forms of address, not insults (depends on the tone, of course)
  • Don’t assume the whole country is Buenos Aires. Córdoba, Rosario, and the north will correct you

Best time to visit

Southern Hemisphere: the seasons are reversed December is summer, July is winter. If you are coming from the north, do not pack as if Christmas meant snow: here it is heat, beach, and asado. One trip will not hit “the perfect month” for both Iguazú and Ushuaia — pick a region. The Argentine year splits more by dry/wet, altitude, and wind than by four European seasons. Weather figures are orders of magnitude, not an SMN forecast.

Below, the places I actually went to plus useful extras (falls vs Puerto Iguazú, whales, ski vs lakes). Tafí, Trevelin, Iberá, Formosa, and each Córdoba valley are tips, not pins. Seasonal prices: indicative ARS + USD at ~ARS 1,550/USD (BNA, Nov 2026); confirm the year.

How to read the country at a glance

  • Northwest (Salta, Jujuy, Catamarca, western Tucumán): dry May–Oct, clear skies, freezing nights at altitude. Dec–Mar is rain, mud, and occasional road closures. Go up slowly: 2,000–3,500 m is real.
  • Litoral / jungle (Misiones, Entre Ríos, Formosa or Iberá if you go): humid year-round. “Winter” is the bearable window; summer is an oven with storms.
  • Cuyo (Mendoza): dry, valley heat in summer, skiing in winter. Harvest is March. Cristo Redentor into Chile shuts with snow.
  • Atlantic Patagonia: wind outranks the thermometer. Whales have a window; the rest of the year the sea stays cold.
  • Lake vs ice Patagonia: summer = trekking and daylight; winter = skiing or short days. Do not mix both plans into one week.

The calendar that fills hotels (not the weather)

  • 1–15 January — The average Argentine goes to the coast or to Patagonia. Buses, flights, and hostels up. If you can, shift to the second half of the month or to March.
  • Carnival (Feb, moving date) — Gualeguaychú and Corrientes fill. The northwest has village carnavalitos, not a sambadrome. Not the same product.
  • Easter week (Mar–Apr) — Domestic tourism: northwest, Cuyo, sierras, hot springs. Book ahead. Hotel prices are not a Tuesday in May.
  • Winter school break (~two weeks in July) — Skiing in Bariloche and Mendoza; cultural CABA; Iguazú stays open (“cool” jungle). Cerro Catedral pedestrian day ~ARS 90,000 / ~USD 58; the skier pass in 2026 press ~ARS 160,000 / ~USD 103 — confirm at catedralaltapatagonia.com.
  • Long weekends — A Friday–Monday stretch fills Carlos Paz, Tigre, Colón. Not national high season; a stretched weekend.
  • Bariloche school-leavers — Mostly Sep–Nov (sometimes from Aug). Clubs and some hostels. Jan–Mar is a different crush: families and foreigners.
  • 8 Dec – 1 Jan — CABA empties toward the coast. The city becomes walkable; many neighbourhood restaurants shut between Christmas and New Year. Confirm bookings.

AMBA and the coast

  • CABA / Buenos Aires — Ideal Sep–Nov and Mar–May: walkable sidewalks, San Telmo fair, fewer storms. Dec–Feb: humid 30–35 °C, afternoon downpours, outdoor bars; the city does not close. Jun–Aug: cool, grey, rarely under 5 °C. “Porteño winter” is a coat, not an Ushuaia parka. Events: Tango Festival (Aug, milongas and shows; many milongas charge a cover, not a stadium ticket); Lollapalooza at the Hipódromo de San Isidro (Mar — confirm that year’s tickets; a day festival in BA is several dinners, not a coffee). Mataderos fair on Sundays works better in drier months.
  • Buenos Aires Province — Tigre almost year-round (the Delta does not close; summer = more boats and more people). Tandil and the small sierras better Oct–Nov and Mar–Apr. Avoid January if the plan is RN2 to the coast: it fills. La Plata is a city day year-round.
  • Mar del Plata — Ideal Nov or Mar: beach without January’s two-week crush. January is the average Argentine, full buses, higher prices. Sea climate: summer ~18–27 °C with cold water; winter harbour, wind, many hotels shuttered — a different city, not a dead resort if you like an ugly sea. The bus usually beats the plane (see the fare network).

Centre, Cuyo, and the Paraná

  • Rosario — Ideal Sep–Nov and Mar–May, walkable waterfront. Dec–Feb is humid river heat (~20–32 °C). Winter is dry and short. The city works without a “postcard season”; the Paraná rises or falls with the year, not with your suitcase.
  • Córdoba — Two cards. The capital (the one I marked) works almost year-round: summer 32–36 °C dry with a siesta; winter ~5–18 °C and sun. Better Mar–May and Sep–Nov. Nueva Córdoba at night, fernet, cuarteto. The sierras are another climate and not pins: Punilla (Carlos Paz ~40 km / ARS 6,500; La Cumbre ~90–100 km; Capilla ~110 km / ARS 21,000), Alta Gracia (~36 km), Calamuchita, Traslasierra. Ideal Sep–Nov and Mar–May; January in Carlos Paz is a crush. Cosquín folklore = last week of January; Cosquín Rock = February; VGB Oktoberfest = October. Circuits and fares on Córdoba · sierras. Condorito APN ARS 25,000 / ~USD 16.
  • Mendoza — Ideal Oct–Nov and Mar–Apr (wineries; harvest around March, central acts at the Frank Romero Day Greek theatre). Dec–Feb 35–40 °C in the shade in Luján de Cuyo: you can drink the wine; daytime trekking suffers. Winter = skiing at Las Leñas/Penitentes: different logistics, different bag, not the wine plan. Cristo Redentor to Chile shuts with snow (~90 days closed in 2026). High Andes (Aconcagua, provincial park): better Dec–Mar; permits extra, not an improvised day trip.
  • San Juan / Ischigualasto — Northern Cuyo, stone moon. I have not been. Same dry logic as Mendoza; the valley of the moon pays off more in autumn–spring than in January’s oven. Not a visited pin.
  • Entre Ríos — Hot springs year-round (Federación, Villa Elisa, Colón). The river is nicer Sep–Nov and Mar–May. Gualeguaychú carnival Jan–Feb: a paid corsódromo — confirm the year; it is not a free sidewalk afterparty. January: mosquitoes and steam. The Uruguay corridor has no “bridge season”: it has immigration. El Palmar (APN) ARS 25,000 / ~USD 16 general fee.

The north

  • Santiago del Estero — Ideal May–Sep: the heat eases and the springs pay off. Dec–Feb is among the hottest provinces (40 °C+ and a mandatory siesta). Chacarera year-round; the National Chacarera Festival is a winter/spring event depending on the edition — confirm dates, do not build the trip only on a poster.
  • Tucumán — Ideal Apr–Jun and Aug–Oct. Dec–Feb: humid yungas heat and afternoon storms. Valley city; 80 km away the air already changes. Tafí del Valle (~2,000 m) is cooler year-round — a tip, not visited. Aconquija bus ~ARS 18,000–20,000 / ~USD 12–13 (Jul–Nov 2026).
  • Salta — Ideal Apr–Jun and Sep–Nov (dry, clear days, ~15–25 °C by day in the valley). Dec–Mar: wet-season rain and heat. Cafayate in summer hits hard (sun at 1,700 m). Cachi and Cardones: a jacket at night even if you sweated at noon. Evening peñas (La Casona del Molino and others) work better in the dry, when more people are in the valley. Los Cardones National Park ARS 25,000 / ~USD 16.
  • Jujuy — Ideal May–Oct in the Quebrada: clear skies, freezing nights, altitude. Dec–Mar: rain and occasional closures. Purmamarca/Tilcara/Humahuaca are not “the same weather”: every 400 m of elevation changes the coat. A jacket after 6 p.m. is not optional. Tilcara/Humahuaca carnival (Feb) is village and comparsa, not Gualeguaychú. 1 August is Pachamama: a ritual, not a ticketed festival. Calilegua (yungas) ARS 25,000 / ~USD 16; more jungle than puna, better in the dry.
  • Catamarca — Valley (San Fernando) hot Dec–Feb (easily 35–40 °C); better Apr–Jun and Sep–Nov. The west (Fiambalá, Belén, Piedra Pómez) is high desert: dry, cold nights, wind. Same northwest logic. I have been; I did not keep a day-by-day diary. Paso de San Francisco into Chile exists; I am not inventing timetables I have not checked.

Misiones and the humid north

  • Misiones / Iguazú — Jungle always humid; the park is open year-round. Ideal Apr–Jun and Aug–Oct: less of an oven, solid water volume. Dec–Feb is hottest (~21–33 °C and humidity that does not forgive) with storms. “Winter” does not close the falls: it closes less of the hotel air-conditioning. APN general fee ARS 60,000 / ~USD 39 (residents ARS 25,000) from 1 Jun 2026. Jungle Gran Aventura and boats are extra.
  • Puerto Iguazú vs the park — Town is the base (heat, Brazil/Paraguay edges). The falls are the jungle-and-boardwalk day: go early; Garganta sometimes closes with high water. Not the same “season” as the avenue shops: the park has a train, humidity, and a queue at 10 a.m.
  • Iberá (Corrientes) — Not visited. Colonia Carlos Pellegrini, 4×4, capybaras, caimans, mosquitoes. Better in the dry (Apr–Oct). January is steam. Corrientes carnival is a different, urban film (Jan–Feb). National Chamamé Festival, January in the capital. Not a map pin.
  • Formosa / Bañado La Estrella — Not visited. Wetland, heat, its own logistics, Paraguay next door. The “less bad” window is dry autumn–winter; summer is an oven. Confirm local operators if you insist — I am not inventing a safari.

Patagonia

  • Puerto Madryn / Valdés — Southern right whales Jun–Dec (2026 season from 9 Jun; peak Sep–Oct). Penguins more like Sep–Mar. El Doradillo, from the shore, Jun–Oct. Do not plan a Feb–May “whale safari”: they are not there. Wind can shut Puerto Pirámides any month. Protected-area fee for foreigners ~ARS 50,000 / ~USD 32 (2026 season) plus the boat. Trevelin / Esquel is the other Chubut (Andes, not whales) — a tip, not visited. ~4–5 h from Bariloche on RN40.
  • Bariloche — Two products. Lakes/trekking Dec–Mar (best Jan–Mar; Nov–Dec is school-leavers). Ski Jun–Sep. Do not expect both in one trip. Autumn (Apr–May) quieter and wetter. Lake climate: summer ~6–23 °C, winter ~−2–8 °C. Circuito Chico works most of the year if the road is not under snow (Turisur ~ARS 40,500 / ~USD 26, 2026 sheet; APN park ARS 35,000 / ~USD 23). National Snow Festival is winter: confirm dates. Villa La Angostura would already be Neuquén: I did not mark it.
  • El Calafate — Perito Moreno “works” most of the year (boardwalks). Ideal Nov–Mar for daylight. May–Aug: cold, short days, some outings get cut by wind. January is expensive and packed; February is still high (summer packages sometimes add an airport transfer). APN park ARS 50,000 / ~USD 32; glacier bus ~ARS 56,000–60,000 one way (Plataforma10, 3 Nov 2026). Hielo y Aventura Mini trekking ARS 420,000 / ~USD 271 (from 1 Nov 2026, without transfer). Kayak: Patagonia Chic ARS 370,000–510,000 / ~USD 239–329 (promo vs list, Nov 2026); free cancel ~24 h on their public policy — verify live. Nearby Chaltén is another trip (I did not mark it): ~2 h 40, better Oct–Apr for trails.
  • Ushuaia — Trekking and the Beagle: Dec–Mar (penguins Oct–Mar). Ski (Cerro Castor): Jun–Aug. Jun–Jul if the idea is a trail: wind and ~7 h of light; Vinciguerra still runs year-round. Summer ~6–14 °C; a jacket, twelve months. APN park ARS 40,000 / ~USD 26. Taun (the local operator I used): Laguna Esmeralda ARS 140,000 / ~USD 90; Vinciguerra ARS 190,000–235,000 / ~USD 123–152 (Taun card 190,000 Nov 2026; third-party agencies sometimes higher). In 2025 that glacier day sat on the order of a few hundred thousand ARS (a range, not an invoice). The short Beagle (sea lions/lighthouse) runs more months than the penguin boat.

If you only have 10–14 days

  • Apr–Jun or Sep–Oct — Northwest (Salta + Jujuy + a hop to Catamarca or Tucumán) or Iguazú plus a litoral edge. CABA as a stop always closes the loop.
  • Mar or Nov — Mendoza wine, or the coast without January, or Calafate/Ushuaia with light and less of January’s crush.
  • Dec–Mar — Lake or ice Patagonia. Do not stuff the Quebrada and Ushuaia into the same “because it is summer” flight: in Jujuy it is rain.
  • Jun–Aug — Skiing (Bariloche or Mendoza) or whales (Madryn) or dry northwest. Iguazú still works, more bearable. Not the month for a long Calafate trek.

The one you will hear most

Rehilated yeísmo: lluvia and yo sound like “sh” or “zh.” A tune that rises at the end of the sentence. Che, boludo (not always an insult), re as an intensifier (re lindo). The accent of series, AM radio, and Ezeiza. Uruguay speaks a variant; it is not “another language.”

Cordobés

Córdoba city and sierras

Its own sing-song

Vowels stretch, the sentence sings. A porteño spots it in two seconds. Cuarteto and fernet are culture, not souvenirs. In the sierras the tune continues; do not expect textbook rioplatense.

Cuyo

Mendoza, San Juan (I have not been to the latter)

Drier, sometimes more “Chilean” to a foreign ear

Voseo still. The cadence can sound more Andean than porteño. In a winery you will hear slower Spanish than in a Palermo bar.

Northwest

Salta, Jujuy, Tucumán, Catamarca, Santiago

Slower, a clearer “s”

Nomás, diminutives, Quechua/Quichua loans in Santiago and the puna (yapa, chaqui). In the Quebrada, Spanish lives next to Quechua and Aymara: do not assume everyone wants to practise English. Tucumán talks faster than high-altitude Jujuy.

Litoral

Entre Ríos, Misiones, Corrientes, Formosa

Guaraní in the ear

Loans: angá, gua’u, tape. Corrientes and Misiones show it; Entre Ríos less so. In Iguazú, Spanish mixes with border portuñol: not a mistake, the triple frontier. Iberá and Formosa: I have not been; the accent still exists.

Patagonian

Bariloche, Calafate, Ushuaia, Madryn

A mix of migrants

A lot of tourist service Spanish sounds more “neutral” to a foreigner. Underneath: people from BA, Chile, and the interior. Hotel-ear in Ushuaia or Calafate is not a Chubut corner store. There is no “penguin accent.”

Music that helps the place click

Two floors first, the rest after. Folklore is the interior: it is not one genre (zamba, chacarera, carnavalito, cueca, vidala). Cuarteto — Córdoba

Not cumbia, not folklore, and not “inland music for tourists.” It is how Córdoba hears itself: the tunga-tunga, a piano that pushes, a couple-dance that starts late. Skip it and you miss the city. A porteño who says “that is just cumbia” is wrong; a cordobés will correct you. Fernet + cuarteto is the night’s default, not a gag. Cosquín folklore (January) and Cosquín Rock (February) are different products: do not mix them with this.

What it is (and where it comes from)

It started in the 1940s with Cuarteto Leo: piano (Leonor Marzano), accordion, violin, double bass. “Cuarteto” is that lineup, not “four hits.” The pulse is called tunga-tunga. You dance it in pairs, at a baile — you do not watch it from a table. La Mona Jiménez (el Mandamás) is the living floor: if this genre has a Gardel, it is him. Rodrigo took it off the provincial map and put it in Luna Park. Today Ulises Bueno, Q’Lokura, La Konga, Tru La La, and Cachumba fill rooms; Luck Ra is the bridge toward urbano. Córdoba cultural heritage, not a terminal souvenir.

The night: bailes, not a dinner-show

A cuarteto baile is local culture. People go to dance, not to film a chorus. It starts late: before midnight a lot of doors are still warm — same as the rest of a Córdoba night. Quality Espacio (the Quality complex, ring road) is the big room a visitor can find: confirm the bill, do not assume La Mona is on that week. La Plaza de la Música and capital / Nueva Córdoba clubs fill the rest. I am not locking “the” after: pesos and doors move. If it is not your thing, there is rock and electronic; they are not the default. Neighbourhood detail on the Córdoba page.

Rodrigo “El Potro” — why he still plays

Rodrigo Alejandro Bueno, city of Córdoba, 24 May 1973. The nickname is El Potro. He started recording at 17 (La foto de tu cuerpo, 1990). In 1991 he planted himself in Buenos Aires (Fantástico Bailable): cuarteto stopped being “a Córdoba thing” for part of the country. Lo mejor del amor (1996, Magenta) made him national; an ACE award came with it. Cuarteteando (recorded in Córdoba) has Ocho cuarenta and Y voló, voló. In 1999, the album El Potro. In 2000 he sold out Luna Park thirteen times and sang La mano de Dios to Maradona. On 24 June 2000, at 27, he died in a crash on the Pan-American highway coming back from a show in Greater Buenos Aires. I am not writing the crash thesis: the fact is public, the rest is asado rumour.

Why he still matters: he is the bridge a porteño, a Rosarino, or a Uruguayan used to hear cuarteto without going to a neighbourhood baile in Córdoba. Twenty-five years on he still sits at more than a million monthly Spotify listeners — not an uncle’s nostalgia. 24 June is named the way Gilda is named: popular floor religion, not a little documentary. Soy cordobés is an identity anthem; if you are in Córdoba on a Saturday you will hear it. He is not “the only” cuarteto singer (La Mona is still on stage; Ulises and La Konga fill rooms now). He is the one who got the genre off the provincial map.

Where to start (I name what still rotates, not a 1999 ranking): Ocho cuarenta, Lo mejor del amor, Soy cordobés, Amor clasificado, Fuego y pasión, Cómo le digo, Qué ironía, La mano de Dios, Y voló, voló. Live: La leyenda continúa / Luna Park if you want the shout, not the studio. Listen: Rodrigo on Spotify · This Is Rodrigo.

Historical figures you will hear about

Not a school manual and not a ranking of heroes. It is “which names you will hear in a taxi, at an asado, or in a plaza,” and why the table sometimes splits. San Martín and Belgrano are the almost-common floor. Güemes is the north San Martín needed. Sarmiento is the school and the other pole of Rosas. Roca and Rosas are where the conversation leaves the bronze and becomes the country of now, projected onto the 19th century. Six short cards, traveler tone. If you want a thesis: Pigna and Historias Innecesarias live under Voices. I am not inventing classroom anecdotes.

1778–1850 · the one almost nobody fights over

José de San Martín

Independence · Andes

He crossed the Andes from Mendoza (1817) with the Army of the Andes and pushed the independence of Chile and Peru. He died in Boulogne-sur-Mer; the remains came home in 1880. The school nickname is “Father of the Nation”: he is in every town, in every Plaza San Martín.

For the country today: he is still the name that works as common ground. 17 August is a holiday. The bronze version is quiet on purpose — that is why he organizes more than Rosas or Roca.

Why people argue (mildly, and among historians). Guayaquil: what he and Bolívar actually said. Whether he leaned toward a constitutional monarchy. The French years. That is a fight in books, not paint on the statue. For a visitor: he is the monument people do not egg.

Where to see him: the tomb in the Metropolitan Cathedral (Plaza de Mayo, right aisle — the CABA walking tour usually passes; no separate ticket). In Mendoza, Cerro de la Gloria (Parque San Martín) is the Army of the Andes monument. Plaza San Martín in Retiro is the photo horse.

1770–1820 · the flag

Manuel Belgrano

Flag · Tucumán and Salta

A lawyer and economist who ended up a general. He raised the flag on the Paraná, opposite what is now Rosario (1812). He won Tucumán and Salta. He was at the 1816 Congress. He died poor on 20 June: that is why Flag Day falls on that date, not the first hoisting.

For today: the flag is still the symbol that can hold an asado together. Also the idea that the country needed schools and an economy, not only an army. You will see him more in a classroom than on a march.

Why people argue (gently). Historians cut the military myth down to size; school made him a saint with a flag. Nobody throws paint at him the way they do at Roca. If 20 June turns into a fight, it is this year’s politics — who gets on the podium — not 1812.

Where to see him: the National Flag Memorial in Rosario (Santa Fe 581; the hall and crypt are usually free, the tower is paid — confirm on the site). The monument crypt is a tribute; the remains are in the Santo Domingo convent (Defensa, CABA). In Tucumán, the Casa Histórica is 1816, not a Belgrano-only museum.

1843–1914 · the map of the south

Julio A. Roca

Modern state · “desert”

President twice (1880–86, 1898–1904). He led the campaign schoolbooks called the Conquest of the Desert: the south and Patagonia under Buenos Aires’ control. Generation of the ’80s: he federalized the city, railways, the agro-export boom of wheat and beef. The map you fly over between Bariloche and Calafate is, in part, that campaign’s map.

For today: towns, routes, and the memory of the 100-peso note. The “rich” Argentina of the 1900 textbooks is assembled here. So are land fights in Patagonia that did not leave.

Why people argue (this one is a street argument). One reading: he closed a violent frontier, built a state, and the model that made the country an exporter. The other: the desert was not empty. Mapuche, Tehuelche, Ranquel peoples — dispossession, killings, land to friends of the regime. “Conquest of the Desert” is the school name for a campaign against people who were already there. The statue on Diagonal Sur sometimes has paint on it. The old 100-peso note with his face became a fight and went away. The name is still on the avenue and on a city in Río Negro. Both things are the conversation. I am not picking a side in a taxi.

Where to see him: the monument on Diagonal Sur / Av. Presidente Julio A. Roca, two blocks from Plaza de Mayo (CABA). The landscape of the argument is the Patagonia in this guide — Bariloche, Madryn, Calafate — not a single museum.

1793–1877 · the one who splits the table

Juan Manuel de Rosas

Federal · Buenos Aires

Governor of Buenos Aires and, in practice, of the Confederation (1829–32, 1835–52). A caudillo. He styled himself Restorer of the Laws. Urquiza brought him down at Caseros (1852). He died in exile in Southampton. Palermo’s woods — Parque 3 de Febrero — sit on what used to be his quinta.

For today: interior versus the port, strongman versus a constitution, “who writes the textbook.” The asado version of federalism (the provinces vs Buenos Aires) still runs this film, sometimes without knowing it.

Why people argue (and argue badly). The liberal school (Mitre, Sarmiento) left him as a tyrant: the Mazorca, obligatory portraits, a delayed constitution, a personalist regime. Twentieth-century revisionism flipped it: he held the country together, faced the Anglo-French blockade at Vuelta de Obligado (1845), spoke for the interior against porteño unitarios. The “tyrant” was written by the winners of Caseros. The textbook flipped more than once. The fight you hear at an asado is about the country each person wants now. I am not rehabilitating or condemning him in a paragraph.

Where to see him: Palermo and Parque 3 de Febrero in CABA (the park, not a Rosas museum). Vuelta de Obligado is in San Pedro, Buenos Aires province: a historic site, not a pin I marked as visited. The National Historical Museum (San Telmo) has a 19th-century room if you want objects, not a verdict.

1811–1888 · the school

Domingo F. Sarmiento

Schools · Facundo

From San Juan, president (1868–74), a teacher before a general. He wrote Facundo (1845) against Rosas: “civilization and barbarism.” He built schools, teacher colleges, libraries. He died in Asunción; they brought him to Recoleta. San Juan’s airport carries his name. I have not been to San Juan: the birth house is a tip, not a pin.

For today: every town has a Sarmiento school. 11 September is Teachers’ Day because he died that day. If someone says the sovereign has to be educated, they are quoting this film. He is the other pole of the Rosas card, not a leftover name.

Why people argue. School made him the saint of the blackboard. Facundo painted the interior as barbarism: in the provinces that still lands. He also wrote harshly about Indigenous peoples. One reading: the man who bet on schools and a literate state. The other: the man who looked at the country from Chile and Buenos Aires and called “desert” what did not fit. Both belong. This is not Roca’s paint fight; it is who writes the textbook.

Where to see him: the tomb in Recoleta Cemetery (CABA; map at the gate, confirm hours). The Sarmiento Historical Museum is in Belgrano (Cuba / Juramento; free, Tuesday–Sunday — confirm). San Juan, if you ever go: the birth house. Not a pin in this guide.

1785–1821 · the north

Martín Miguel de Güemes

Gauchos · northern frontier

From Salta. He ran the gaucho war against royalists coming down from Upper Peru. That frontier holding is part of why San Martín could cross the Andes: the north did not collapse on him. Governor of Salta. He was wounded in an ambush and died on 17 June 1821. Salta’s airport is named for him: the name is waiting when you land.

For today: in Salta he is not “one more prócer.” He is the city’s. 17 June feels different there than in a Buenos Aires classroom. If the plan is the northwest, this name organizes more than Roca.

Why people argue (gently, and more in Salta than in a Palermo taxi). For years the national textbook kept him small next to San Martín; Salta kept him huge. One reading: the caudillo who held the frontier with montoneras. The other: the gaucho myth (Lugones and company) that left more bronze than person. Not a paint fight. It is “who tells the north’s independence.”

Where to see him: the equestrian monument at the foot of Cerro San Bernardo (Av. Uruguay and Paseo Güemes, Salta). The hill stairs start behind it; the hill is the same outing. The remains are in Salta Cathedral (Pantheon of the Glories of the North, since 1918). I am not inventing crypt hours: confirm on the ground.

Plaza de Mayo names San Martín. Recoleta, if it is on the plan, has Sarmiento. Güemes makes sense in Salta, at the foot of the hill. You do not have to agree with the guide.

One last tip

Argentines can be intense, direct, and full of opinions (about soccer, politics, asado, anything). But they’re also warm, generous with food and time, and they’ll welcome you well if you come with good vibes.

Argentina has its quilombos — inflation, chaotic politics, bureaucracy — but it also has incredible landscapes, excellent food, and people who will go out of their way to make you feel at home.

Enjoy it. And bring dollars. 😉
